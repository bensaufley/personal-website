I've had this on my list for a long, long time, and finally got around to it. Having been written during the W era, it took me back, in places. But it's also maddening how not _only_ is everything just as true now as it was then—Davis actually expressed some optimism for change, at times, and knowing that the _opposite_ happened really drives the point home. I can't remember the specific quote but there was one that was kind of, "perhaps _just how bad it's gotten_ today will wake people up to the need for change," and that didn't happen.

I read this book already knowing about much of the worst aspects of the modern prison system and the prison-industrial complex (though not all! a number of illuminating anecdotes and data points); I was hoping to learn a little more about the "what else?" question. This book doesn't particularly address that: the last chapter or so talks a lot about what can be done about the evils of the current system, but it mostly relies on imprisoning fewer people and breaking down the systems that rely on imprisoning people. I agree with all that, but the book doesn't do _much_ for the tricky question of "what about the worst offenders?"

I think it's a really important book, and I'd recommend it to anyone who wants to learn more about the problems at play in the US prison system and beyond, and how we got here; but it's going to be just one book in my educational journey here.
